When you start a company, founders do everything. There were five of us at Tinybird, and my part was clear at the beginning: rebuilding the UI, building the demo apps, and hiring.

## When the team grows, the holes multiply

Molly Graham has a famous idea about scaling startups: [give away your Legos](https://review.firstround.com/give-away-your-legos-and-other-commandments-for-scaling-startups/). As the company grows, you have to hand your pieces to new people so they can build, and you go find new ones. My spin on it is a bit different. Every time the team grew, new holes appeared, and someone had to plug them. All of us founders did it, each in our own way. I became a basketball player who lives for rebounds: not the one who scores, but the one who grabs every loose ball so the team gets another chance.

## You're not there to do what you like

As a founder, you're not there to do what you like. You're there to cover what's needed. Over seven years that meant a real medley: UI, demos, hiring, billing, security, product, data, internal tools, and whatever else had nobody looking after it.

The trick is to savour each task, even the ones you never pictured yourself doing. And to lead teams from the mud, next to them, not from above. Some days my main job was moving tickets around 😄

## Four holes that marked me

*The money*

I never thought I'd lead a team that handled billing for the whole platform. I did it twice. At the beginning, we only used our own platform to calculate each user's usage in real time and sent the numbers to Stripe. It worked, but when you use your own product, you find things. You stop, fix, change, and then keep going. Dogfooding, right? Painful, but it pays off in the long run.

In the next iteration we brought Orb in, but kept pulling the metrics from Tinybird, again. It was the best decision we made: build on our own system, but use a billing platform built for enterprise. That second time, it worked because I was fully focused on the problem. Let's be honest: plugging several holes at once is normal in a founder role, but that doesn't make it ideal, or mean you'll do everything else well.

*Oh, security*

I ended up there because nobody else was. After someone left, the seat was empty, and instead of hiring for it, I took it myself. I didn't realise how big it was. At the same time I was also covering billing and product. Then came everything around certifications: complying with them, passing audits, taking care of the annual pentesting, filling in security questionnaires, improving the CSP (the browser security rules) of the website and the app, applying for grants, and keeping track of every external service we used, who used it, and why...

*The data*

It sounds obvious now, but back then our data was split by region, then by workspace. Only engineers could answer questions about our users. Not even support had that data at hand, and marketing or sales could forget about it. So we built Operational Analytics in under ten days, with a team of two, and later [Birdwatcher](https://www.tinybird.co/blog/birdwatcher-slack-app), an agent that brought those answers to everyone.

*Letting people go*

Nobody is ready to let someone go, not even professionals. It's just who I am: I believe a person can always be won back, that there's always more you can bring out of them (not more hours, I mean more of what they're good at), and that part of the blame sits on our side too. It's the most painful and ugliest task there is, though I'd never compare my pain to that of the person on the other side.

Only one thing makes it worse: letting someone go for reasons that have nothing to do with their performance. That really means you, as a founder, didn't do your job well. You didn't see it coming, or the results didn't arrive.

One of the things that hurt most, and took me longest to accept, is that you stop being a teammate to everyone else. In the end, you're their boss, or one of the founders. That never stopped me from trying to know the person before the employee, and from staying close. It made those letting-go conversations even harder, because you know what's behind them. But I wouldn't change that for anything in the world. First human, then employee.

For me, that also meant being there when people came to the office from out of town. Talking to them, asking how they were, spending time with them. Going remote was one of the best decisions we ever made, but the moments we spent together were key, and they deserve to be treated that way.

## Find the space, then hand it over

As five founders, we learned to find (or accept) the spaces we could fill, and then hand them to someone far more qualified, someone better, when the time came. I don't think that gets mentioned enough. You have to be ready for the day you change roles, adapt, learn, push, and start all over again.
